The 300: a park within 300 metres

Does everyone live within walking distance of a park or wood? From every entrance of a park or wood we calculated how far you walk in 5 minutes over real streets and paths. Every home in that zone meets the rule (green on the map). For information, the 15-minute zone was calculated too.

Zuid-Holland, weighted by the number of homes per building. Per municipality from 51.0% (Nieuwkoop) to 95.1% (Zoeterwoude) within 5 minutes.

These are the map's figures, computed with FME. There is now a new calculation with open software only, a walking distance per home and stricter green: 36.8% of homes within 300 m, 56.1% within 5 minutes. See the new calculation.

What is measured?

The paper asks for a large public green space within 300 m of every home, "approximately a 5-min walk". We measure those 5 minutes of walking over the network of streets and paths, counted from the entrances of the green.

What the result assumes

The paper leaves open from where and how far to measure. The choices below fill that in and make the most difference to the figures. Each says which way it pushes the result; the reasons are under "Choices and why".

  • A small park counts. Green counts from 300 m²; the WHO norm behind the rule is 1 ha. (more favourable)
  • Touching the zone is enough. If part of a building lies in the 5-minute zone, all its homes pass, also for a long block of flats with only one end inside. (more favourable)
  • Walking starts at an entrance. Green without a path into it has no entrance and does not count, and a shortcut missing from OpenStreetMap is missing from the route. (stricter)
  • 5 minutes is about 425 m along streets, roughly 300 m as the crow flies, with water, railways and motorways as barriers.
  • Public access is not checked. A path into the green is taken as access. (more favourable)

Steps

  1. Collect the green. Parks and woods from OpenStreetMap and from TOP10NL (Kadaster), combined (as of 2024).
  2. Leave out pieces that are too small or too narrow. Only green of at least 300 m² and with a perimeter/area ratio of at most 0.35 remains. That second condition removes long narrow strips, such as verges: a strip has to be about 6 m wide or more. This green is on the map (from neighbourhood level): dark green outlines, filled in once the homes show. Click it for its type, source and area.
  3. Find the entrances. All paths and streets you may walk on (OpenStreetMap, within 300 m around every municipality) are intersected with the edge of the green. Where a path enters the green is an entrance: 27,111 in total.
  4. Calculate the walking zones. From every entrance, an isochrone calculator (Valhalla) uses a network analysis over the streets and paths of OpenStreetMap (pedestrian profile) to calculate the area you reach on foot in 5 and in 15 minutes. All zones are merged.
  5. Classify the homes. A BAG building with a residential use that lies in or on the edge of a 5-minute zone meets the rule: green on the map. All other homes are red. Whether a red home lies within 15 minutes is shown in the popup.
  6. Per municipality, district and neighbourhood. The share of homes within 5 (and 15) minutes, on the same areas as the 3 and the 30. A building counts for as many homes as it has addresses with a residential use.

Choices and why

ChoiceWhyAlternative we did not choose
Walking time over the network 300 m as the crow flies can in reality be a long detour, because of water, a railway or a motorway without a crossing. The paper itself speaks of "approximately a 5-min walk". A circle of 300 m (buffer): simpler, but ignores barriers.
5 minutes The network analysis (Valhalla) uses 5.1 km/h for pedestrians by default: 5 minutes is about 425 m along the road. With the usual detour of roads compared with the straight line, that comes to around 300 m as the crow flies. Exactly 300 m over the network: stricter than the WHO's 300 m as the crow flies.
Colour by 5 minutes only The rule is 300 m, about a 5-minute walk: a home meets it or not. The map therefore only colours green (within 5 minutes) and red (further). A separate colour for 5–15 minutes: suggests an in-between category the rule does not have.
15 minutes as extra information Shows whether homes that miss the 300 are just outside or really far from green. Visible in the popups and as a walking zone, not in the colours. Leaving it out altogether.
From the entrances You enter a park by a path. Counting from the edge would also count fences, water and dense woodland edges as access. Distance to the edge of the green.
At least 300 m², not too narrow Small planted patches and verges are no place for recreation; the shape condition removes strips that are large but narrow. The WHO threshold of 1 ha (see limitations).
OSM and TOP10NL Both sources miss pieces: OSM is strong in parks and paths, TOP10NL in woods and areas outside the city. One source: more gaps.
A building counts if it touches the zone The front door is somewhere on the facade; if part of the building lies in the zone, the green is within reach. Only the building's centre point: stricter for large buildings.
Open-source isochrone calculator Free, reproducible and with a pedestrian profile over OpenStreetMap. Commercial routing services: costs, and not repeatable.

Is this right for my home?

Per neighbourhood, district and municipality the map gives a reliable picture. Per home it is a good indication, but not a measurement on site. Is your home red while a park seems close by? Then it can be because of this:

  • The route is longer than the straight line. We calculate a 5-minute walk over existing streets and paths. If there is water, a railway or a busy road without a crossing in between, a park that looks close can still be a longer walk.
  • The entrance is on the other side. We calculate to an entrance where a path enters the green, not to the edge. A park with a fence or ditch on your side counts from the nearest entrance.
  • The green does not count. A lawn without paths, a narrow green strip or a private garden is not a park in this calculation. Newly created green may still be missing from the map sources.
  • A path is missing in OpenStreetMap. A shortcut you always take yourself, but which is not on the map, is unknown to the network analysis.

Whether a red home lies within 15 minutes is shown in the popup.

New: the 300 with open software

The 300 has been computed again for the whole province, now entirely with OSGeo software from OSGeo4W, the same installation as QGIS. GDAL finds the entrances of the green and builds the walking network from OpenStreetMap, GRASS GIS computes the walking distances, and QGIS shows the result. Every home gets a walking distance in metres to the nearest entrance of a park or other public green, so 300 m, 5 minutes and 15 minutes become filters on one result. As a check, Valhalla, the route planner of the old calculation, computes the same again.

The map still shows the FME calculation described above. It moves to the new method once open water has been cut out of the green (see "Still to do" at the end of this part).

Which green counts

Three sources: the parks of the provincial facilities map (OpenStreetMap and TOP10NL, peildatum 17 September 2025); from TOP10NL the functional areas park, recreation area, nature area, national park, estate, heemtuin and botanical garden; and from TOP10NL also cemeteries and allotments. Duck decoys do not count: most are closed to visitors. Pieces under 300 m² and narrow strips drop out. What overlaps or touches becomes one green area, so a path from one piece of green into the next is not an entrance. This gives 1,891 green areas with 11,270 entrances.

Expansion: what you reach on foot

Valhalla can return every street and path you reach on foot from a point, with the walking distance to each piece. This is called expansion. Below, from the Provinciehuis on the Zuid-Hollandplein in The Hague.

GRASS does essentially the same, from all entrances at once: every junction of the walking network gets the shortest walking distance to an entrance. A home gets the distance of the nearest point on a street, plus the short stretch from the home to that street.

Why no more zones

The old calculation drew an area around the streets reached, the isochrone, and counted every home inside it. Such an area is coarse: it also covers the inside of blocks, water and railway land, which the walk goes round.

In the province, 46.4% of homes lie inside the 300 m zones, but 38.1% are really within a 300 m walk.

Cycle paths count as walking routes

Dutch traffic law lets pedestrians use a cycle path where there is no pavement or footpath. The calculation therefore lets pedestrians walk on cycle paths; bridleways do not count. Valhalla's default profile closes cycle paths, so Valhalla gets its own rules with one change: cycle paths are open to pedestrians, unless OpenStreetMap tags them foot=no. This matters: in Delft, with the old green, 87% of homes are within 300 m with the default profile and 91% with cycle paths.

GRASS and Valhalla compared

Both get the same entrances, the same homes and the same OpenStreetMap data. They agree for 97.8% of homes on whether they live within 300 m of green. For 71% of homes the distance differs by 10 m or less, for 93% by 50 m or less.

Where they differ, Valhalla's walk is shorter more often. Valhalla also reaches a home over its own private or destination-only road; the network for GRASS leaves out roads tagged access=private and links the home to the nearest public way in a straight line.

Compared with the FME calculation

FME counted 79.1% of homes within 5 minutes, GRASS 56.1%. Per home they agree for 69%. Most of the difference is the green: the FME calculation counted everything on its green map, including small woods, scrub, gardens, golf courses and sports grounds. The new sources only hold parks, nature, recreation areas, estates, cemeteries and allotments. The rest of the difference comes from the zones.

A path along such a shore then becomes a park entrance. Open water will be cut out of the green first; after that the map moves to this calculation.

Limitations and open issues

  • 300 m² is much smaller than the WHO norm of 1 ha. The paper and the WHO mean a large public green space; here a small park counts too. The result is therefore more favourable than under the strict norm. A calculation with 1 ha is the first improvement.
  • Public access. Whether the green is publicly accessible has not been checked; an entrance only means that a path enters the green.
  • Green without paths (an open lawn by the street) gets no entrance and therefore does not count.
  • Made with FME, with an isochrone calculator (Valhalla) that ran locally for a while. The steps use open data and open software; the workbench is in the repository, but only runs with FME. The new calculation with open software only is above.
