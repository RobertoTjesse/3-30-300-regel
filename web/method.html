Measuring the 3-30-300 rule for every home in Zuid-Holland

How we turned a three-line guideline into numbers for 1.8 million homes, 2,509 buurten and 50 gemeenten, and how far those numbers can be trusted.

Roberto BethOpen-source pipeline: Python + GDAL, FME, PostGIS, ValhallaCoordinate system RD New (EPSG:28992)

Summary

Konijnendijk (2023) proposed that everyone should see three trees from home, live in a neighbourhood with 30% tree canopy, and have a park within 300 m. We computed all three for the province of Zuid-Holland from lidar surface models, a tree register, a canopy map and a pedestrian network. Most homes see three trees and most are a short walk from green. Canopy cover is the rule the province is furthest from.

385.9%of homes see at least 3 trees within 30 m; 5.0% see none
308.1%of the land lies under a crown; 102 of 2,509 buurten reach 30%, no gemeente does
30078.9%of homes are within a 5-minute walk of a park or wood; 99.2% within 15 minutes

1The rule and how we read it

The paper states each part in one sentence and leaves the measurement open: from where you look, how far, what counts as a tree or a park. The table below gives the paper's wording and the definition we used. Section 7 lists every choice and which way it moves the result.

3Every citizen should be able to see at least three trees from their home. Measured as the number of trees whose crown top has a clear line of sight to a point 1.8 m above the ground just outside the facade, counting trees up to 30 m from the trunk.
30Every neighbourhood should have at least 30% tree canopy cover. Measured as crown area from the BKB 2024 lidar raster divided by land area, per CBS buurt. Water is left out of the land area.
300Every home should be no more than 300 m from the nearest park or green space. Measured as a 5-minute walk (about 425 m over paths and streets) from a park entrance to the building, for green of at least 300 m².

2Method: the 3

For every 0.5 m cell of the AHN surface model we count how many trees can see it. A viewshed is computed from each tree's crown top with a 30 m radius, and the visible cells of all trees are added up. A home then takes the highest count found in a 1.5 m ring around its building.

2.1Line of sight

The observer sits on the crown top: the highest surface point within 1.5 m of the tree point. Placing it on the tree point itself puts the observer inside its own crown, and in Delft 35.5% of trees then saw practically nothing. The target is 1.8 m above the ground, roughly eye height at the front door. Anything in the surface model blocks the view, including hedges, fences, cars and other crowns.

2.2From cells to homes

Inside a building footprint the surface model shows the roof, so the count there means nothing for someone at street level. The score of a building is the highest count in a ring of 0.5 m cells from the facade to 1.5 m outside it. One good spot is enough: if a corner of the back garden sees three trees, the building passes. Every home in the building gets that score, so a block of 80 flats weighs 80 times in the area totals.

2.3Pipeline

The province is cut into 500 m tiles with a 35 m halo, more than the 30 m radius. Trees and terrain are read over the halo, so a tree just across a tile or municipal edge still counts, but only the inner 500 m is written back. The tiles then fit edge to edge without seams.

Of the 5.2 million tree points, 1,275,676 (24.5%) are marked in the register as “disappeared, small tree” or “not seen once, small tree”. They are left out. The effect on the result is shown in Figure 9.

3Method: the 30

Canopy cover is the share of land under tree crowns. The crowns come from Boomkroonbedekking 2024 (BKB 2024), a 0.25 m raster made from lidar in which 1 means crown. The areas are the CBS buurten of 2025, the same ones the web map uses.

canopy coverbuurt = Σ crown cells × 0.0625 m²land area (CBS, water excluded) × 100% (1)

A cell belongs to the buurt that holds its centre. A crown on a boundary is therefore split between buurten and never counted twice, and overlapping crowns count once because the ground under them exists once. A wijk or gemeente value is the crown area of all its buurten divided by their total land area. The buurt is the level the paper asks for, and a large green buurt can lift a gemeente while its other buurten stay far below 30%.

4Method: the 300

A home counts when it lies within a 5-minute walk of the entrance of a park or wood. Green comes from a combined OpenStreetMap and TOP10NL green map (2024). An entrance is a place where an OpenStreetMap path crosses the edge of the green. From each entrance the Valhalla routing engine draws the area a pedestrian reaches at 5.1 km/h, along streets and paths, around water, railways and motorways. A building counts when any part of it lies inside that zone.

Two filters decide what counts as green: at least 300 m², and not a narrow strip. A verge or a hedge row has a lot of edge for its area, so shapes with a perimeter-to-area ratio above 0.35 m⁻¹ drop out. For a long strip that ratio tends to 2 / width, which puts the cut-off at a width of about 5.7 m.

5Results

The province as a whole: 85.9% of homes see three trees or more, 8.1% of the land lies under a crown, and 78.9% of homes are within a 5-minute walk of green. No gemeente meets the 30. Wassenaar comes closest at 27.9%, Kaag en Braassem is lowest at 2.5%.

6Validation and benchmarks

The 3 has the most room for error, so most of the checking went there. It was compared with ArcGIS Spatial Analyst, with an exact line-of-sight test on single trees, and with the earlier benchmark raster for Delft. The 30 was compared with the earlier FME calculation and with Cobra Groeninzicht.

6.1Viewshed engine against ArcGIS and an exact test

The original ArcGIS benchmark for Delft turned out to have its observers at twice the intended height: the elevation field had been given as both observer elevation and observer offset. With an isolated tree and an isolated group of trees, ArcGIS with those settings reproduced the benchmark on 100% of cells. The benchmark was then rerun with the intended observer.

The pixel assignment of the 30 was checked on Delft's 91 buurten: the counted area differs from the polygon area by at most 4.1 m², and summing buurten gives the same wijk and gemeente totals as clipping those directly, to within 0.02 m².

6.4Run times

The 3 is the heavy part: 10,241,760 viewsheds over 28,723 tiles. Trees near a tile edge are computed by both neighbours, which is why that number is higher than the number of trees. All 52 surface-model areas took 25.6 hours on one workstation with 4 workers. The 30 takes about 4 minutes for the province (6,225 tiles of 1 km, 5 processes).
